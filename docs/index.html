      How the break was found

      First, clean 9,011 daily prices.

      Two date formats share one file, “20-May-87” and “Nov 14, 2022”. Each row is parsed, sorted, de-duplicated and given a log return.

      Then let PyMC weigh every month as the break.

      A uniform prior over 427 monthly averages, one mean either side and a Normal likelihood. Every parameter converged with R-hat at 1.00.

      Finally, line the break up with what happened.

      The nearest curated events sit two to six years away, so February 2005 reads as a slow change of regime rather than one shock. An independent ruptures search lands on April 2005.

      This study finds when Brent’s behaviour changed. It cannot prove why. When a break lands near a war or an OPEC meeting, that is a coincidence in time, and I report it as consistent with, not caused by.

      Markets also move before the news. Traders priced in the Iraq invasion for months before March 2003, so a break can lead its event. Two events can share a window too, as COVID and the Saudi–Russia price war did in March 2020.

      The model is deliberately simple: one break, two means and a Normal likelihood. Daily returns have far heavier tails than a Normal allows, with excess kurtosis near 66, and inventories, the dollar and interest rates sit outside the model entirely.

      Next I would fit several breaks at once, swap in a Student‑t likelihood, and add GDP, exchange rates and inflation as covariates.

      The goose behind the name

      The benchmark is named after the Brent oilfield, 186 km north-east of Lerwick in the Shetland Islands. Shell named its North Sea fields after seabirds in order of discovery: Auk, Brent, Cormorant, Dunlin. Brent is the brent goose.

      The field has since been lifted out of the sea. Brent Delta’s topside was carried to Hartlepool in 2017 and broken up for recycling. The price kept the name.
